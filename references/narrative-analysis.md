# Narrative analysis

## Build the narrative DNA

Extract evidence from the supplied film material before inventing imagery.

```text
Surface story:
Deep theme:
Protagonist:
Conscious desire:
Hidden need:
Opposing force:
Irreversible turn:
Emotional aftertaste:
Recurring objects:
Recurring spaces:
Recurring actions:
Natural imagery:
What the audience may understand only after watching:
```

Compress the result into one dramatic proposition:

> A person who wants **X** must confront **Y**, only to discover **Z**.

Do not expose `Z` in public-facing art when it is a major reveal.

## Score motif candidates

Generate 6–10 candidates drawn from the story, then score each from 1–5:

| Criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| Story specificity | Could this motif belong only to this film or a narrow set of films? |
| Symbolic range | Can it carry both literal and emotional meaning? |
| Silhouette | Is it readable at thumbnail size? |
| Transformation | Can it support a visual metaphor operation? |
| Cultural grounding | Is its meaning appropriate to the story's place and period? |
| Spoiler safety | Can it intrigue without revealing the turn? |
| Cliché penalty | Is it already overused for this genre? |

Select one primary motif. Add at most two supporting motifs only when they clarify the relationship rather than decorate it.

## Control disclosure

- **Teaser**: show the question, hide the answer.
- **Main poster**: show the conflict and emotional promise.
- **Character poster**: show a distinct desire, wound, or relationship.
- **Festival poster**: privilege theme and authored point of view.
- **Post-release poster**: allow a deeper image that rewards viewers who know the film.
